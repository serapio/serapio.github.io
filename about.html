01

The short version

Computational linguist, evaluator, and writer. The kind of linguist who wants to know how the data was annotated.

I take language systems apart for a living: ASR, NLU, LLM features, multilingual data pipelines, and the evaluation machinery that is supposed to tell us whether any of it works. I care about evidence, reproducible methods, and writing down the awkward findings instead of averaging them away. The awkward findings are usually the useful ones.

02

How I got here

I started with physics and linguistics at UC Santa Barbara, then a master’s in computational linguistics at San Diego State, where I worked on the classic NLP tasks: corpus building, sentiment analysis, information extraction, and discourse modeling. My PhD at UC San Diego focused on probabilistic models of language variation, the sound systems of low-resource languages, and statistical modeling of speech acoustics. Most of that work was in languages that do not get enough attention: Ixpantepec Nieves Mixtec, Choguita Rarámuri, Inhambane Gitonga, and Jinhua Wu.

Fieldwork taught me to distrust tidy data. Industry taught me that the same lesson scales. At MindMeld I worked on search quality and conversational assistants; at Cisco I led data development, evaluation, and product metrics for voice and language features, including Webex Assistant for Rooms and Webex Meeting summaries. At Kedara.care I built an eldercare assistant and, more importantly, the evaluation hooks that made it observable.

Now I consult on evaluation and audits for language-based products, with a focus on healthcare. The work is part measurement, part diagnosis, and part translation between the people who build a system and the people who have to decide whether to trust it.

03

What I care about

Principles, not a personality quiz. These are the things that tend to decide whether an evaluation is useful.

Evidence

Measure the thing you are deciding

A metric is only useful if it bears on a real decision. I would rather have a small, honest evaluation than a large dashboard nobody trusts.

Language

Look at the language, not just the average

Multilingual systems fail unevenly. Aggregates hide the interesting part, which is usually a specific language, domain, accent, or discourse context doing something the model cannot handle.

Craft

Write it down so someone can reproduce it

Methods, annotation guidelines, failure taxonomies, and limitations are part of the result. If nobody else can rerun the evaluation, it is a story rather than evidence.

Use

Make the result useful to the team

The best evaluation changes what someone builds next. That means plain language, prioritized findings, and a clear account of what to fix first.
